Knowledge Has a Half-Life — Decay Begins the Moment You Stop Learning

Knowledge behaves like a radioactive element: it does not fail suddenly; it decays exponentially. Every half-life, half of it turns into nonsense.

Half-lives differ by field. News has a half-life of twenty-four hours; framework documentation, eighteen months; engineering knowledge, about ten years; medical knowledge, seven — the fields with the shortest half-lives are precisely the ones running fastest. Mathematics counts its half-life in millennia, because mathematics keeps only what does not rot.

So "finished learning" is a state that does not exist. A degree is not a container of knowledge; it is a snapshot from the day you enrolled — snapshots yellow, and the darkroom closes.

The mechanism is a single line: the metabolism of knowledge is not a defect, it is the engine. Old conclusions being overturned means the field is breathing; a shrinking half-life means civilization is accelerating. An engineer complaining that knowledge expires is really complaining that the profession is alive.

Three corollaries. First: the core of education is not transmitting facts but installing the habit of updating them — four years cannot teach a forty-year career, but it can teach how to relearn weekly. Second: personal knowledge is a flow, not a store — the level is held up by continuous inflow; stopping learning is not standing still, it is an exponential ebb. Third: organizations need a cold chain for knowledge — documentation, retrospectives, handover rituals are all, in essence, refrigerated logistics that extend knowledge's life.

Only one part of knowledge is immortal: methodology. Facts rot; the method for replacing facts is its own preservative.

Knowledge has a half-life. There is exactly one way to stay fresh: keep flowing.
